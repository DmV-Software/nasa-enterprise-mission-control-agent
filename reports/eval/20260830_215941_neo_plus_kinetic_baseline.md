### How Kinetic Energy of an Asteroid Impact is Estimated

To calculate the kinetic energy ($KE$) of an asteroid impact, scientists use the classical physics formula:

$$KE = \frac{1}{2} m v^2$$

Where:
*   **$m$** is the mass of the asteroid (in kilograms).
*   **$v$** is the relative velocity of the asteroid at impact (in meters per second).

#### Step 1: Estimating the Mass ($m$)
Because we rarely know an asteroid's exact mass, we estimate it using its estimated diameter ($d$) and an assumed density ($\rho$) based on its composition (usually rocky or metallic).
1.  **Volume ($V$):** Assuming a spherical asteroid, $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ (where radius $r = d/2$).
2.  **Density ($\rho$):** 
    *   Chondritic/Rocky asteroid (most common): $\sim 2,000 \text{ to } 3,000 \text{ kg/m}^3$
    *   Iron/Metallic asteroid: $\sim 7,000 \text{ to } 8,000 \text{ kg/m}^3$
3.  **Mass ($m$):** $m = V \times \rho$

#### Step 2: Calculating Kinetic Energy ($KE$)
Once mass and velocity are known, they are plugged into the $KE$ equation. The result is in Joules ($J$). 

To make this number easier to comprehend, scientists convert Joules into **Megatons of TNT equivalent** (1 Megaton of TNT $\approx 4.184 \times 10^{15}$ Joules). For comparison, the Hiroshima nuclear bomb was roughly 0.015 Megatons, and the Chelyabinsk meteor impact of 2013 released about 0.5 Megatons of energy.

### Threat Ranking Scales

If an asteroid has a non-zero probability of impacting Earth, it is ranked using two primary scales:

#### 1. The Torino Scale (0 to 10)
This is a tool designed for public communication. It categorizes threats based on impact probability and potential damage (kinetic energy).

| Torino Rating | Color Code | Description |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **0** | White | **No Hazard:** The likelihood of collision is zero, or the object is too small to reach the ground intact. |
| **1** | Green | **Normal:** A routine discovery of an object passing near Earth that poses no unusual level of danger. |
| **2–4** | Yellow | **Meriting Attention by Astronomers:** Close encounters with a 1% or greater chance of collision, capable of causing localized destruction. |
| **5–7** | Orange | **Threatening:** Close encounters with a significant threat of regional or global devastation. |
| **8–10** | Red | **Certain Collisions:** Impacts are certain to occur. Damage ranges from localized destruction (8) to global climatic catastrophe (10). |

#### 2. The Palermo Technical Impact Hazard Scale
This is a logarithmic scale used by specialists to quantify and rank very small impact risks over long periods. 
*   A Palermo score of **less than -2** indicates the event has no practical consequences.
*   Scores between **-2 and 0** indicate situations that merit careful monitoring.
*   Scores **greater than 0** indicate a threat that is higher than the background hazard level (the average risk posed by objects of the same size over the years).
